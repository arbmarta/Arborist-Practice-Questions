Energy: photosynthesis and respiration

Photosynthesis uses light energy to form glucose from water and carbon dioxide, releasing oxygen. Glucose can be used immediately, stored as starch, or converted into compounds such as cellulose, lipids, amino acids, and other carbohydrates.

Respiration releases usable energy from carbohydrates using oxygen. Trees depend on the balance between photosynthesis and respiration to support growth, defense, transport, reproduction, and storage.

Photosynthesis can be restricted by shade, drought, temperature, chlorophyll content, soil oxygen, and nutrient deficiency.

Leaves, stems, and roots

Leaves are major sites of photosynthesis and gas exchange. Transpiration is water-vapor loss, primarily through leaf stomata, and is a major driver of water movement through the tree.

Stems support the crown and contain conducting tissues. Cambium is meristematic tissue that produces phloem outward and xylem inward, increasing stem and root diameter. Sapwood contains functional xylem; heartwood is older inner wood.

Roots provide absorption, conduction, storage, gas exchange, and support. Fine absorbing roots depend on suitable oxygen, moisture, and soil conditions. Mycorrhizae are symbiotic associations between fungi and absorbing roots.

Primary and secondary growth

Primary growth increases length and occurs at meristematic growing points such as buds and root tips. Secondary growth increases girth through cambial activity. Earlywood and latewood reflect different portions of the annual growth cycle.

Water uptake and osmosis

Osmosis is movement of water through a semipermeable membrane from higher water potential toward lower water potential. Root structure regulates the movement of water and dissolved materials into vascular tissues.

Compartmentalization

Compartmentalization of decay in trees describes four walls that resist spread after injury. Wall 1 resists vertical spread, Wall 2 inward spread, Wall 3 lateral spread, and Wall 4 separates newly formed tissue from injured or decayed tissue. Wall 4 is the barrier zone formed after injury.

Palms are different

Palms are monocots. They do not have a vascular cambium like typical woody trees and do not undergo the same secondary growth. Their stems contain vascular bundles, and growth depends on a single primary apical meristem.
