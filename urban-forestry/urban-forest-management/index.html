Why urban forests matter

Urban trees can improve air quality, manage stormwater, aid infiltration, reduce erosion, store carbon, provide shade, reduce heat and wind, support wildlife, reduce energy demand, and contribute social, economic, and health benefits. They also carry costs and conflicts: planting and maintenance expense, risk, infrastructure conflicts, pollen and volatile compounds, debris, screening, and invasive-species impacts.

Measuring the resource

Tree inventories organize information about individual trees and populations. Urban tree canopy describes the area covered by tree crowns when projected onto the ground. Canopy mapping can reveal distribution patterns and support planning, while tree-equity analysis considers whether benefits are distributed fairly across neighborhoods.

Plans, standards, and specifications

Urban forest management can include planting, preservation, maintenance and operations, removal and replacement, risk management, emergency response, outreach, and community engagement. Specifications translate broader requirements into detailed procedures for work.

Maintenance and emergency response

Young-tree structural pruning can reduce later defects and maintenance burden. Storm response requires organized priorities, safe operations, and decisions about damaged trees and infrastructure.

Wood waste

Wood can be chipped, retained as wildlife habitat where appropriate, reclaimed, converted to biochar, or used as biomass fuel. Disposal choices matter because anaerobic decomposition can produce methane and burning releases stored carbon.
