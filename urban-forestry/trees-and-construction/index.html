Urban Forestry

Protecting retained trees through planning, design, construction, and post-construction care, with attention to roots, soil, grade, and exposure.

Construction can start a spiral of decline

Construction can injure trunks and branches, sever roots, compact soil, change grades, alter drainage and soil chemistry, and suddenly expose formerly sheltered crowns. Effects may appear well after construction is complete.

Tree protection zones

Terms such as dripline, critical root zone, and tree protection zone help define areas where impacts should be limited. The appropriate protected area depends on tree condition, species tolerance, site constraints, and the work proposed.

Root cutting and soil compaction

Root cutting can be selective or non-selective, but location, root size, amount removed, tree condition, and species tolerance all affect consequences. Soil compaction reduces pore space and therefore water infiltration, oxygen availability, and root growth. Construction specifications for buildings and roads often demand compaction levels that are hostile to tree roots.

Grade changes and exposure

Adding fill or cutting soil can alter oxygen, moisture, and rooting conditions. Removing neighboring trees or structures can also expose a retained tree to wind and sunlight conditions for which its existing form did not develop.

Development phases

Planning evaluates trees for preservation and considers where structures might go. Design creates plans, specifications, and protection zones. Construction executes those plans while impacts occur. Post-construction care includes monitoring and addressing changed soil and water conditions.

Tree preservation works best when arboricultural input occurs before site design is fixed, not after construction has already begun.
