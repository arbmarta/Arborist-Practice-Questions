Disorders & Disturbances

Abiotic disturbances are noninfectious problems caused by nonliving environmental, site, or mechanical factors. Their patterns often provide the first clue to diagnosis.

Stress and disturbance

A disorder is an abnormal condition that impairs plant function. Abiotic agents are nonliving and noninfectious. Disturbances may be acute, occurring suddenly with rapid damage, or chronic, developing over long periods. Lightning and untimely frost are acute examples; poor drainage and soil compaction are chronic examples.

Acute

Sudden onset and nearly immediate damage. Think storms, lightning, rapid freeze, or mechanical injury.

Chronic

Slow onset over time. Think compaction, drainage problems, persistent site limitations, or recurring stress.

Look for patterns

The course contrasts biotic and abiotic patterns. Abiotic injury can affect multiple genera or families exposed to the same condition and may appear relatively uniformly across a plant or site. Biotic agents are more often host-limited and may begin in isolated portions of a plant.

Check: Several unrelated tree species along one newly graded strip decline at the same time. Which category should be high on your list?

An abiotic site disturbance. A shared pattern across unrelated hosts suggests examining grading, drainage, compaction, chemicals, or other site-wide factors before assuming a host-specific pest or pathogen.

Mechanical and soil-related injury

Examples in the course include root and trunk damage, soil compaction, nutrient deficiencies, flooding, and girdling roots. These problems can disrupt water and mineral uptake, gas exchange, vascular transport, or structural integrity.

Weather and environmental injury

Rapid freeze, ice storms, hurricanes, sunscald, lightning, and heat can injure trees directly. Timing and distribution matter: ask what weather event occurred, when symptoms appeared, and whether similarly exposed plants show comparable injury.

Other abiotic agents

Competition and allelopathy, pollution, and chemical injury are additional noninfectious causes. Diagnosis requires connecting the observed pattern with site history and exposure rather than relying on a single symptom.

Diagnostic principle: symptoms such as scorch, chlorosis, wilt, or dieback describe what the tree is doing; they do not by themselves identify the cause.
