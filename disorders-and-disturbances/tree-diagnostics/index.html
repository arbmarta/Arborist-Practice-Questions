Disorders & Disturbances

Good diagnosis is a process of narrowing possibilities. Start with the plant and pattern, examine the whole site and tree, and resist treating a symptom as a diagnosis.

Signs versus symptoms

Signs are direct indications of a causal agent and can be diagnostic—for example galleries or conks. Symptoms are effects produced in the plant, such as wilting or necrosis, and often have multiple possible causes.

Describe before you diagnose

Useful symptom vocabulary in the course includes blight, blotch, canker, chlorosis, decay, dieback, gall, gummosis, necrosis, oozing, scorch, spot, stippling, stunting, vascular discoloration, wilt, and witch's broom.

A six-step diagnostic process

  1. Accurately identify the plant. Many issues are host specific.
  2. Look for patterns of abnormality. Compare the plant with what is normal for the species and season; ask whether other plants are affected.
  3. Carefully examine the site. Determine whether affected plants are restricted to an area and what has changed on the site.
  4. Note foliage colour, size, and thickness. Look for premature colour change, twisting, curling, or abnormal development.
  5. Check the trunk and branches. Look for discoloration, drying, oozing, loose bark, wounds, emergence holes, and reduced growth.
  6. Examine roots and the root collar. Check trunk flare, constricting roots, root condition, soil moisture, and evidence of anaerobic conditions or disease.

Work systematically and rule out possibilities. Trees often have more than one problem, so a “spiral of decline” may be more realistic than a single-cause explanation.

Root clues

The course emphasizes that many problems arise underground. Healthy roots should be evaluated for flexibility, firmness, and colour. Brown roots can be associated with dry soil or toxic chemicals; black roots can indicate anaerobic conditions, disease, or pathogens.

Laboratory analyses

When symptoms alone are insufficient, sample the affected area and provide the host identity, history, and surrounding site conditions. For suspected pathogens, sample the transition between diseased and healthy tissue. Insects and mites can be placed in a sealed vial, jar, or plastic bag; leaves or branches can be wrapped in dry paper towel and sealed in a plastic bag; mushrooms and fruiting bodies should be kept in dry paper bags or boxes.
