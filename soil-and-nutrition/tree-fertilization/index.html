In a complete fertilizer, the three primary nutrients are nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). The N-P-K label, also called the guaranteed analysis, shows the percentage of nutrients in the product by weight.

This activity helps you practice the math used in determining fertilizer application amounts.

Using fertilizer as a treatment

Fertilizers can increase nutrient supply and may be used to address a diagnosed deficiency. The course material emphasizes that application should be timed with root growth and that sufficient soil moisture is needed. Fertilization is therefore a treatment decision, not a substitute for diagnosing why a tree is performing poorly.

Soil analysis can provide information about plant-available nutrients, organic matter, pH, and cation exchange capacity. Foliar analysis measures nutrient content in foliage. These results, together with site conditions, can help determine whether added nutrients are likely to address the problem.

Application methods

MethodCourse notes
Surface applicationDry fertilizer may be spread and liquid fertilizer sprayed on the soil surface. It is easy and low-cost, but nutrients can run off or volatilize, and trees may compete with turfgrass.
Subsurface applicationDry fertilizer can be drilled or liquid fertilizer injected into the root zone, improving distribution and uptake.
Foliar sprayApplied to leaves for rapid plant use, but the effect is temporary and material may drip through the canopy.
Injection or implantMaterial is introduced through drilled holes into the tree. The course limits this use to micronutrients, cautions against use on drought-stressed trees, and notes that drilling wounds the tree.

Before applying fertilizer

Misuse can cause fertilizer burn. The lecture recommends considering slow-release fertilizers and using soil—and where appropriate foliar—analysis to identify deficiencies. Soil pH and drought conditions should also be considered before treatment.

Check: Why should chlorosis not automatically trigger fertilization?

Because nutrient symptoms can result from problems with availability or uptake, including soil pH, drought, compaction, waterlogging, and root or vascular problems. Testing and site diagnosis should come before treatment.

Nerd-to-know note on N-P-K labels

On the label, nitrogen is listed as actual N, but phosphorus and potassium are usually reported as available phosphoric acid (P2O5) and soluble potash (K2O). This means the label tells us how much of each labeled nutrient is in the fertilizer, not the exact elemental amount of phosphorus or potassium.

For example, a guaranteed analysis of 30-15-10 means that the bag contains 30% N, 15% P2O5, and 10% K2O.

How to find the amount of each nutrient

We can calculate how many pounds of each nutrient are in the bag by multiplying the total bag weight by each percentage and dividing by 100.

Nutrient weight = Total bag weight × Nutrient percentage ÷ 100

Worked example

A 20 lb bag of complete fertilizer with a guaranteed analysis of 10–6–3 contains 10% nitrogen, 6% available phosphate, and 3% soluble potash. The amount of each nutrient in the bag is calculated as follows:

  • Nitrogen: 20 × 10 ÷ 100 = 2.0 lb
  • Available phosphate: 20 × 6 ÷ 100 = 1.2 lb
  • Soluble potash: 20 × 3 ÷ 100 = 0.6 lb

In other words, the numbers on the bag tell you the percentage of each nutrient by mass, which can be converted into the actual weight of nutrient in the product.

You can also simplify the calculation by using the decimal form of the percentage instead of dividing by 100. Since 10% is the same as 0.10, you can multiply the bag weight directly by 0.10 to get the nutrient mass. This is a faster way to do the same calculation.

Worked example

A 10 lb bag of complete fertilizer with a guaranteed analysis of 20-10-5 contains the following amounts of nutrients:

  • Nitrogen: 10 × 0.20 = 2.0 lb
  • Available phosphate: 10 × 0.10 = 1.0 lb
  • Soluble potash: 10 × 0.05 = 0.5 lb
