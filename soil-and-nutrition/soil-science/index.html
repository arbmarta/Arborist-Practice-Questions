Soils & Nutrition

Soil is more than a rooting medium. Its physical structure, chemistry, biology, and water relationships determine how effectively a tree can obtain oxygen, water, and mineral elements.

How soils form

Five broad factors influence soil formation: climate, organisms, relief or topography, parent material, and time. Climate includes precipitation and temperature; organisms include vegetation, microbes, soil animals, and people; relief includes slope, aspect, and elevation; parent material is the unconsolidated material in which soil develops; and time allows these processes to act.

Climate

Precipitation and temperature influence weathering and biological activity.

Organisms

Plants, microbes, soil animals, and humans all participate in soil development.

Relief

Slope, aspect, and elevation alter drainage, exposure, and local conditions.

Parent material + time

Soil develops from parent material as weathering and biological processes operate over time.

Soil horizons

A soil profile can contain several distinct horizons. The O horizon is an organic surface layer containing materials such as leaves, twigs, bark, and organisms. The A horizon is rich in soil organic matter and contains many absorbing roots. An E horizon, where present, is a zone of mineral weathering and eluviation and contains less organic matter than the A horizon. The B horizon is a zone of accumulation. The C horizon is partially weathered parent material above unweathered material or bedrock.

Check: Why is the A horizon especially important to arborists?

It contains much of the tree's absorbing root system and is rich in soil organic matter, making conditions near the soil surface especially important for tree health.

Soil physics

Organic matter

Soil organic matter consists of dead plant and animal material. It changes with moisture, helps bind mineral particles together, and can be increased through practices such as adding mulch or compost.

Texture

Soil texture describes the relative proportions of sand, silt, and clay. Sand particles are the largest, clay particles are the smallest, and the balance among the three strongly affects water holding, aeration, compaction, nutrient supply, and other soil behaviour.

Structure, aggregates, and pore space

Primary soil particles can group into aggregates, which give soil structure. Flocculation groups particles through interparticle forces, while cementation stabilizes those groups using agents such as organic matter and clays.

Pore space is just as important as the solid particles. Macropores are relatively large spaces that promote aeration and drainage but are too large to retain much water against gravity. Micropores are smaller and retain water between precipitation events. Coarse soils tend to be dominated by macropores; fine soils contain more micropores.

Bulk density is the weight of dried soil per unit volume. High bulk density is associated with low porosity. This relationship is particularly important when assessing compacted urban soils.

Soil chemistry

pH and buffering

Soil pH measures acidity or alkalinity on a scale from 0 to 14. The scale is logarithmic: a pH of 5 is ten times more acidic than a pH of 6. The course material identifies approximately 6.0–6.5 as favourable for plants.

Buffering capacity is a soil's resistance to pH change. Soils high in clay and soil organic matter generally have greater buffering capacity. Sulfur can lower pH and lime can raise it, although their effects may be limited spatially and temporally.

Cations and cation exchange capacity

Anions carry a negative charge and cations carry a positive charge. Cation exchange capacity (CEC) describes a soil's ability to attract, retain, and exchange positively charged cations. Clay and organic matter have high negative charge density, so clay-rich and organic soils tend to have higher CEC.

A higher CEC is associated with greater soil buffering, greater retention of nutrients in available forms, and greater retention of some contaminants.

Saline soils contain excessive salts. Sodic soils are high in sodium and may develop crusting, high pH, nutrient deficiencies or imbalances, and poor structure.

Soil biology and roots

Soil is biologically active. The course highlights mycorrhizal fungi and soil fauna, with fauna grouped as micro-, meso-, and macrofauna. Roots grow where conditions are favourable and require space, organic materials, mineral elements, water, and gas exchange. Many absorbing roots occur close to the soil surface.

Water in soil

Water movement depends on texture and the arrangement of soil layers. In a sand-over-clay profile, water can accumulate in the sand because it percolates slowly into the clay. In a clay-over-sand profile, a capillary barrier can cause water to accumulate in the clay until the upper layer becomes saturated enough for water to move into the sand.

Check: Why can adding a contrasting soil layer create drainage problems?

Water does not necessarily move freely across a sharp textural boundary. Differences in pore size and percolation can cause water to accumulate above the boundary.

Compaction and urban soils

Compaction reduces total pore space and can make it difficult for trees to obtain water, oxygen, and mineral elements. Management options presented in the course include air excavation, radial trenching, and tillage.

Urban soils may also have abrupt textural differences, reduced or missing O horizons, altered biological activity, and greater fluctuations in surface soil temperature. Structural soils are engineered to meet building requirements while allowing root growth. Structural cells instead carry structural loads while maintaining spaces for uncompacted soil, though they are costly.
