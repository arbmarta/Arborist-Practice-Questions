Soils & Nutrition

Trees need enough water to maintain physiological processes, but too much water can be just as damaging as too little. Good water management starts with the plant, the soil, and the weather.

How much water does a tree need?

Water needs vary with the plant—including species, size, and growth rate—and with climate, including air temperature, humidity, light, wind, and season. Evapotranspiration combines water lost through plant transpiration and evaporation from the soil.

If transpiration exceeds precipitation and soil moisture reaches the wilting point, irrigation may be necessary.

Drought

Drought conditions provide insufficient water for transpiration and photosynthesis and cause loss of turgidity. Symptoms described in the course include marginal leaf scorch, leaf drop, twig dieback, temporary afternoon wilting, root loss, and—in severe cases—plant death.

Temporary wilting can occur on hot summer afternoons when transpiration exceeds the tree's ability to take up water, with recovery overnight. Adaptations to arid conditions can include deep spreading roots, leaf shedding or temporary dormancy, smaller leaves, thicker cuticles, and sunken stomata.

Flooding

Flooded soils restrict air and gas exchange. Flooding can also leach essential nutrients, contribute to mineral toxicity, and produce gleyed grey or purple-grey soils. Tree symptoms can include leaf yellowing, defoliation, crown dieback, root death from oxygen deprivation, fermentation, rot, and disease. Flooding can also increase risk of tree failure.

Some plants are adapted to wet conditions through specialized root and stem tissues that transport oxygen or through shallow rooting that improves access to oxygen near the surface.

Diagnostic caution: drought or something else?

Symptoms associated with water stress can have lookalikes. The course identifies girdling roots, trunk injury or cankers, vascular wilt disease, insects affecting xylem, and lightning damage as problems that can disrupt water uptake or transport.

The soil moisture reservoir

The soil moisture reservoir is the volume of soil occupied by roots together with the water-holding capacity of that soil. It is lower in sandy soils than in clay soils and lower for plants with small root systems.

Irrigation needs therefore depend on tree species and size, the amount of water stored in the root-zone soil, and environmental conditions.

Irrigation

Newly planted trees

Newly planted trees deserve particular attention because roots may have been lost or damaged during harvesting and planting, and the remaining root system is initially concentrated within the root ball. The course recommends irrigating around the periphery of the root ball, then reducing or eliminating irrigation frequency as roots grow and the tree establishes.

Common systems

SystemCharacteristics and use
SpraySmall droplets and a slow application rate; works best on level sites in calm weather and should be scheduled to infiltration rate and weather.
BubblerStream or umbrella pattern with moderate-to-fast application; used for newly planted trees, often just outside the root ball with a berm to retain water.
DripSlow delivery from hose or tubing; place close to roots, schedule to plant and soil needs, and inspect for leaks or clogged emitters.
Other methodsHigh-pressure water injection, soaker hoses, basin irrigation, and water bags.

Irrigation should not be applied faster than water can infiltrate. Avoid puddling, runoff, and erosion; shallow watering can encourage surface rooting.

Water quality and conservation

Water-quality concerns include salt or nutrient accumulation, disease such as Phytophthora, clogged irrigation nozzles, and pollutants. The course recommends soil salinity testing where appropriate.

Conservation approaches include using only the irrigation needed, xeriscaping with drought-resistant landscapes, grouping plants into hydrozones according to water needs, and monitoring soil moisture before and after irrigation.

Mulch and drainage

Mulch can reduce evaporation, improve soil conditions, reduce weed competition, moderate summer soil temperature, reduce mechanical injury, and support soil biological processes. The lecture recommends a mulch depth of approximately 2–4 inches.

Drain tiles can help address persistent flooding by removing gravitational water, but the course emphasizes that they are not a fix for heavy clay soils or improper irrigation practices.

Check: Why might drainage tiles fail to solve a chronically wet site?

They remove gravitational water. A heavy clay soil or excessive irrigation can still leave the soil too wet even after gravitational water has drained.
