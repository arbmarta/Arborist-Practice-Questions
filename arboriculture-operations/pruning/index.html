Arboriculture Operations

Effective pruning starts with an objective, respects tree biology, and uses cuts that achieve the objective while limiting unnecessary injury.

Start with an objective

Pruning specifications can include clean (remove dead, diseased, or broken branches), thin (reduce live-branch density), raise (provide vertical clearance), and reduce (decrease height or spread). Broader objectives include improving structure, reducing risk, providing clearance, managing crown size, reducing crown density, and restoring structure.

Pruning is not automatically beneficial. It can reduce wildlife habitat and photosynthetic capacity, create sapwood dysfunction and wounds requiring compartmentalization, and—when poorly performed—increase likelihood of failure.

Pruning biology

Trees do not heal damaged wood; they seal and compartmentalize it. Large cuts take longer to seal. Correct branch removal respects the branch collar, branch bark ridge, and branch protection zone. Flush cuts remove protective tissues and are not appropriate.

Timing and dose

Remove only what is necessary to meet the objective. Tolerance varies with age, structure, health, weather, and site; young trees generally tolerate more pruning than old trees. The course notes that no more than one-quarter of mature-tree foliage should be removed under the referenced guidance.

Pruning before bud swell can maximize subsequent growth and support compartmentalization; pruning after bud swell can minimize growth because resources have just been used for new growth. Dead, diseased, and broken branches can be removed in any season. Avoid pruning during drought, and consider pest transmission periods and local pruning restrictions.

Flowering and fruiting trees

Current-season bloomers can be pruned in winter before leaf emergence or in summer after bloom. Last-season bloomers are pruned just after bloom to preserve the next season's flowers. Dormant pruning can emphasize structure; pruning after bloom can reduce fruit production.

Technique

Use sharp, appropriately sized tools; bypass blades are preferred over anvil-type tools. The course advises against pruning while wearing climbing spurs and recommends sterilizing tools between cuts with a non-phytotoxic material or liquid. Use the three-cut method where needed to prevent tearing or splitting.

Types of pruning cuts

CutPurpose / definition
Branch removalRemoves the smaller of two stems or branches at a union; made outside the branch collar and branch bark ridge.
ReductionRemoves the larger of two branches at a union and directs growth to a remaining lateral; the course specifies a lateral at least one-third the diameter of the removed branch.
HeadingRemoves a stem to an internodal point or to a branch less than one-third the diameter of the removed branch; generally ill-advised on mature trees.
ShearingStimulates dormant and adventitious buds; useful in topiary or hedging but does not produce a natural crown form.
Check: A large limb must be shortened while retaining a suitable lateral branch. Reduction or heading cut?

A reduction cut, provided the retained lateral is appropriately sized. Heading cuts terminate a branch at an internode or at an undersized lateral and are generally inappropriate for mature-tree crown reduction.

Structure, clearance, and crown management

Structural pruning can remove dead, diseased, broken, or weakly attached branches, address included bark and overextended branches, and promote a strong leader. Young-tree training establishes a dominant leader, lowest permanent branches, well-spaced scaffold branches, and subordinate temporary branches.

Clearance pruning may address infrastructure, sight lines, activities, other plants, or people. Directional pruning can direct growth away from utilities. Crown elevation removes lower branches, but over-elevation can increase likelihood of failure.

Size management uses reduction cuts (and less commonly heading cuts). Density reduction increases light or wind penetration and promotes interior foliage; lion tailing—removing lower and interior foliage—is malpractice.

Pollarding is not topping

Pollarding deliberately creates pollard heads, allows sprouts to grow from those heads, and repeatedly removes sprouts back to the same heads without damaging them. Topping removes a large portion of the crown with large heading cuts. The course links topping with difficult compartmentalization, weak new attachments, rapid regrowth, increased failure potential, and possible tree death.

Special cases

Palms: remove fronds, flowers, fruit, or loose petioles when they create dangerous conditions; generally do not remove green fronds, and avoid damaging living tissue. Palm wounds do not close over.

Conifers: remove dead, dying, and diseased branches and, where appropriate, restore a dominant leader; generally do not pollard or reduce/head lateral branches.

Shrubs: common objectives include height reduction, rejuvenation, and aesthetics. Shearing is appropriate only for tolerant plants and creates a dense outer crown that can shade interior foliage.

Plant growth regulators

Plant growth regulators can alter growth, flowering, and fruiting. The course notes utility use for maintaining clearance and describes antigibberellins as reducing cell elongation and growth rates, potentially reducing pruning requirements and water-sprout production after pruning.
