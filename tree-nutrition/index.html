Soils & Nutrition

A nutrient can be present in the soil and still be unavailable to a tree. Understanding nutrition means looking beyond the amount of an element to the soil and plant processes that control uptake.

Essential elements

Nutrients participate in tree metabolism and other plant processes. The course notes that plants require approximately 16 to 19 essential elements. Carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen are obtained from the atmosphere, while other elements can be obtained through uptake from soil, internal cycling, and atmospheric deposition such as rainfall and dust.

Macro- and micronutrients

Macronutrients are required in relatively large quantities. The primary macronutrients are nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium; secondary macronutrients include sulfur, magnesium, and calcium. Micronutrients are required in smaller quantities, but can still limit growth.

Nutrient availability

The presence of a nutrient in a soil analysis does not guarantee that a tree can take it up. The course identifies several causes of nutrient problems, including low soil organic matter, acidic or alkaline soil, altered or compacted soil, waterlogging, wilt diseases, cankers, borers, volatilization, and leaching—especially in sandy soils with low organic matter.

Think of nutrient status as an interaction among the nutrient supply, soil conditions, root function, and the tree's ability to transport and use what it absorbs.

Recognizing deficiencies

General nutrient-deficiency symptoms can include reduced growth, small size, and chlorosis. The lecture uses nitrogen deficiency as an example: chlorosis is observed initially on older leaves and, with time, may appear on both young and old leaves.

Check: A soil test reports plenty of a nutrient, but the tree appears deficient. Is that possible?

Yes. Soil pH, compaction, waterlogging, root or vascular problems, and other factors can interfere with availability, uptake, or transport even when a nutrient is present in the soil.

Testing

A soil test can provide information about plant-available nutrients, soil organic matter, soil pH, and cation exchange capacity. A foliar test measures nutrient content in foliage. Used together, these tests can help distinguish nutrient supply in the soil from nutrient status in the plant.

Testing is especially important before assuming that fertilization is the appropriate response to poor growth or chlorosis.

From diagnosis to treatment

Fertilizers can increase nutrient supply and address deficiencies, but the course emphasizes timing with root growth and the need for sufficient soil moisture. Fertilizer choice and application should therefore follow diagnosis rather than replace it.
